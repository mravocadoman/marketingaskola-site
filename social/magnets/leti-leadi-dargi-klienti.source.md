
A cheap lead is not a cheap customer. I learned that from a webinar funnel for an Excel course: €1,175 of ads, more than 2,000 email signups, €0.38 each. For a long time that was the number I showed people.

The other number came out of the same ad account, the same week, the same spreadsheet. Roughly 2% of those signups bought the €250 course. The other ninety-eight percent are still on a list.

Here's the strange part: the campaign was fine. Roughly forty buyers at €250 is about €10,000 of course revenue against €1,175 of ad spend. That's my own sum, before refunds and VAT. Nothing broke and nobody complained.

That's exactly why it nearly taught me nothing. A failure gets investigated. A modest success gets repeated.

**What you'll have at the end:** a way to tell whether your own cheap leads are lying to you, one better number to count instead of cost per lead, and a twelve-question check you can run on your campaign this week.

## What you need

- **Your ad account**, the one your lead ads run from.
- **Your sales records**: your shop, your payment tool, or your CRM (the list where you track leads and deals).
- **A spreadsheet**, or a sheet of paper. No special tools, no analyst, no data warehouse.
- **Money:** nothing extra. It costs attention, and one uncomfortable month.
- **Time:** most of the questions you can answer from memory, in under a minute.
- **Skill:** no coding. One step, sending your sales back to the ad platform, may need whoever set up your website's tracking.

## How it works

Cost per lead is a price, not a result. You can push a price as low as you like by finding people for whom saying yes costs nothing. That's the same group of people for whom saying yes means nothing.

Look at what my €0.38 bought. The ad said "free live webinar" and "learn Excel", to a broad audience, because broad audiences are cheap. The form came prefilled, so signing up took two taps and no decision. Every choice made the front number better. Not one made the back number better.

So who signed up? Mostly people who'd like to be better at Excel the way they'd like to read more: real interest, no €250 behind it. Then reflex taps by people who don't remember signing up. And people who wanted one formula fixed today, not a course. No email or webinar fixes them. They're fixed at the moment you find them, or not at all.

Then the platform makes it worse. Think of it as a brilliant personal shopper with no common sense. Give it a list with one item, and it buys that item as cheaply as it can, a little better every week. If the list says "people who fill in my form", that's what it brings. Nobody told it you sell a course.

<pre class="flow"><b>You tell it</b>  "get me form signups"
     │
     ▼
<b>It learns</b>  who fills in forms, from the people who just did
     │
     ▼
<b>It finds more of them, cheaper</b>  people who say yes to anything free
     │
     ▼
<b>Your dashboard</b>  more leads · cheaper leads · same revenue
     │
     └──▶ back to the start, a bit cheaper every week
</pre>

Each round your cost per lead improves, so you do more of what produced it: broader audiences, lighter ads, less friction. That speeds the loop up. The platform walks away from anyone who hesitates before saying yes, and hesitation is the most reliable sign of real intent there is.

A lead is not a person. It's an action you saw someone take, and it's worth exactly what that action is worth. So count something that costs the other person effort.

## Step 1: run the twelve-question check

Pick a campaign you're running now. Each question says which answer means a number is flattering you.

1. **Is the campaign told to get form signups, not purchases, booked calls or real replies?** If yes, your cost per lead measures the price of easy attention, not demand.
2. **Has your cost per lead improved over the last three months while revenue stayed flat?** If yes, you're not getting better at marketing. You're getting better at finding people who say yes to nothing.
3. **Is your form prefilled, with nothing that makes the person think?** If yes, you're measuring thumb movement. One question that needs a typed answer will raise your cost per lead and improve everything after it.
4. **Do your sales get sent back to the ad platform, matched to the click?** If no, the platform doesn't know revenue exists. It thinks the signup is the goal.
5. **Can you say right now what percentage of last quarter's leads became customers?** If no, your cost per lead is a price paid for something of unknown value.
6. **Does any report you decide from count email opens or clicks as engagement?** If yes, you're counting actions that cost nothing and are often done by machines. Count replies.
7. **Did your best channel get its result in a small market, a narrow niche or at a lucky moment?** If yes, the number describes the conditions, not the playbook. Ask what happens at three times the budget.
8. **Could a completely uninterested person do the main thing your campaign counts, by accident?** If yes, it says nothing about intent. Don't aim the platform at it.
9. **Do you decide whether a lead was "qualified" after you know whether it closed?** If yes, your qualification rate is circular and your forecast is fiction.
10. **Is any headline number a ratio with the real counts left out?** If yes, add them. A 300% improvement on two sales is two extra sales.
11. **Are your results averaged across campaigns, audiences or months?** If yes, you'll underfund your best performer and keep paying for your worst. Averages are where dead campaigns go to look alive.
12. **If your best number turned out wrong tomorrow, would your plan change?** If no, it's a trophy, not a measure. My €0.38 was one for a long time.

Don't fix everything. Fix the answer that touches the most money and run the check again in a month. Yes to question 1 and no to question 4? Jump to step 4. Until that pair is fixed, everything else just speeds the loop up.

**Done when:** the one answer that touches the most money is circled.

## Step 2: decide what a qualified conversation is, before you look

Cost per lead answers "what did it cost to get someone onto my list". The question worth paying for: what does one real conversation with someone who could plausibly buy cost me? I call it cost per qualified conversation. It needs two definitions, written down somewhere other than your head.

**A conversation** is a two-way exchange with a human: a reply you had to answer, a call that happened, a demo that ran. Not an open, not a click. If nobody on your side had to spend attention on it, it wasn't a conversation.

**Qualified** is a filter you write before you know the outcome: the right kind of organisation, a plausible budget, the problem you actually solve, some authority to act. Judge each conversation against it, from what was said. If you call a conversation qualified because it closed, you've built a circle, not a measure.

**Done when:** both definitions are written down, before you've read a single conversation.

## Step 3: count what one qualified conversation costs

The sum is deliberately simple:

<pre class="flow"><b>cost per qualified conversation</b>  =  <b>S</b> ÷ <b>Q</b>

<b>S</b> = everything you spent in the period, including the fees
    and tools you wouldn't pay for if the campaign didn't exist
<b>Q</b> = conversations in the period that met the rules
    you wrote <i>before</i> you read any of them
</pre>

A worked example with made-up letters: S of €4,000 and Q of 20 means €200 per qualified conversation. If you close a quarter of them (C = 0.25), a customer costs (S ÷ Q) ÷ C, which is €800. That's illustrative arithmetic, not figures from any account I've run.

You can't cheat this number by removing friction. Prefilled forms, broader audiences and freer offers add people without adding conversations, so it stays the same or gets worse. A number that gets worse when you cheat is a number you can steer by.

It won't be perfectly accurate, and it doesn't need to be. Count it the same imperfect way every month and the trend is trustworthy.

For contrast, my cold emails for the LIFT digitalisation programme got 127 replies from 4,837 emails, roughly 70% positive. A reply costs the person a minute, which is why that number holds up.

**Done when:** you have the number for last month, and you'll count it the same way next month.

## Step 4: point the platform at something that takes effort

**First, send your sales back.** Each sale has to travel back to the ad platform, matched to the click that brought it. If your sales only live in your shop, payment tool or CRM, the platform can't learn from them. It's the entire prerequisite, and it's where you may want your developer.

**Then change what the campaign aims at.** Switch its goal (the platform calls it the objective) from form signups to purchases. Nothing else needs to change on day one.

The catch: most accounts don't get enough purchases for the platform to learn from quickly, especially high-priced sales to businesses and long sales cycles. Don't go back to forms. Aim at the nearest action that costs the person effort:

<table>
  <tr><th>Action</th><th>Effort it takes</th><th>Aim at it?</th></tr>
  <tr><td>Prefilled form</td><td>Two taps. None.</td><td>No, this is the trap</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Form they type in themselves</td><td>Small but real</td><td>Weak, better than nothing</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Real reply</td><td>Writing a sentence to a human</td><td>Yes</td></tr>
  <tr class="hl"><td>Booked call</td><td>Giving up a slot in their calendar</td><td>Yes, usually the best available</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Purchase</td><td>Money</td><td>Yes, if you get enough of them</td></tr>
</table>

A booked call is the workhorse. Almost nobody hands a stranger a block of their future time on a whim, and it happens far more often than a purchase, so the platform can learn from it.

The rule underneath: **aim at the earliest action in your funnel that an uninterested person wouldn't bother to do.** That sentence is most of what this article is for.

**Done when:** your sales flow back to the ad account, and the campaign aims at something that takes effort.

## Step 5: hold your nerve for a month

Your cost per lead will rise, often sharply, and lead volume will fall. That's the platform refusing to buy you people who were never going to pay. On the dashboard, the fix working looks exactly like the fix failing.

So stop steering by cost per lead. Watch cost per customer, and your number from step 3. If cost per lead doubles and cost per customer falls, you've won. The only casualty is a slide in a monthly report.

**Done when:** a month has passed, and you judged it on cost per customer.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Repeating a modest win.** The obvious next move with the €0.38 campaign was to run it again, bigger, with the same goal and the same easy form. That's the loop, entered on purpose.
- **Taking credit for the market.** A €1,000 test of mine returned 36 times its ad spend in revenue. It was Latvia, under two million people, with unusually good product fit and good timing. Very little of it was skill.
- **Picking a number because it's impressive.** An earlier version of this article carried a spend claim I couldn't stand behind, so I took it down. Every figure here comes from three campaigns: the €0.38 webinar, the €1,000 test and the LIFT emails. Anything else is a worked example. If you find one that isn't, tell me.

## Start here today

Open your main ad campaign and find what it's told to get more of. Then ask one question: could a completely uninterested person do that by accident? If they could, change the goal to the nearest action that takes effort, a booked call, a real reply or a purchase, and leave the cost-per-lead column alone for a month while you watch cost per customer instead.

### The campaign that produced €0.38 leads

The offer was an Excel course, priced at €250. The funnel was the standard webinar shape, and there is nothing clever in it: the cheapness came from the structure being frictionless, not from any skill on my part.

<pre class="flow"><b>Budget</b>  €1,175 total
   │
   ▼
<b>Ad</b>  free live webinar · "learn Excel" · broad interest targeting
   │      <i>objective: lead-form completions</i>
   ▼
<b>Lead form</b>  name + email, prefilled by the platform
   │      <i>no qualification question, no cost to say yes</i>
   ▼
<b>Result</b>  2,000+ email signups · <i>€0.38 each</i>
   │
   ▼
<b>Webinar</b>  free, live, useful
   │
   ▼
<b>Offer</b>  €250 course
   │
   ▼
<b>Bought</b>  roughly 2%
</pre>

Read that diagram as a sequence of prices. Every step before the offer costs the person nothing. A prefilled lead form is the cheapest possible yes in digital marketing: two taps, no typing, no decision. The word "free" appears in the ad. The word "webinar" implies learning rather than buying.

That is not a coincidence and it is not bad luck. Removing friction from a funnel lowers the price of a yes for everyone, and the people most responsive to a lower price of yes are the people with the least intent to do anything after.

The arithmetic, using only the two sourced figures: two percent of 2,000-plus signups is roughly forty buyers. Forty buyers at €250 is roughly €10,000 of course revenue against €1,175 of ad spend. That arithmetic is mine, derived from the two numbers above. It is not a reported revenue figure, and I have not adjusted it for refunds, payment plans, VAT, or the cost of running the webinar itself.

### Who the €0.38 actually bought

The useful question is never "why was the conversion rate low." It is "who exactly did the €0.38 buy me, and what were they doing when they saw the ad." The answer, roughly: people for whom "learn Excel" is an aspiration rather than a problem. That is a real and sizeable want, the kind that happily attends a free webinar on a Thursday evening. It is just not a want with €250 behind it. Underneath that sit three distinct groups, and they fail for different reasons:

  <div class="card">
    <h4>The aspirational</h4>
    <p>Genuinely interested in the topic, genuinely not in the market. They signed up because free costs nothing. They will open your emails for two years and never buy.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="card">
    <h4>The reflexive</h4>
    <p>The form was prefilled and the thumb was already moving. They do not remember signing up. When your email arrives they read it as spam because, from their side, it is.</p>

  <div class="card">

None of these three are fixed by better copy, better email sequencing, or a better webinar. They are fixed at the point of acquisition or not at all.

### A lead is an observed action

The list you build out of the aspirational, the reflexive and the wrong-problem is a list of people who demonstrated exactly one behaviour: willingness to fill in a free form. If that is the only signal you collected, it is the only signal you have, and it is the signal you will unknowingly ask the ad platform to go and find more of.

<div class="callout callout--cyan">
  <p><b>The reframe that makes this concrete:</b> a lead is not a person, it is an <i>observed action</i>. The value of a lead is the value of the action you observed. If the observed action was "tapped a prefilled form for something free," you have bought exactly that, at €0.38, two thousand times over.</p>

### The optimisation trap, in full

It is a loop, it runs on every ad platform, and it is not a bug: the machine is doing precisely what it was told.

<pre class="flow"><b>1 · You set the objective</b>
   "Optimise for lead-form completions."
   <i>This is the only instruction the system ever receives.</i>
        │
        ▼
<b>2 · The algorithm learns</b>
   It builds a model of who completes lead forms,
   from the people who just completed lead forms.
   <i>It is extremely good at this. That is the problem.</i>
        │
        ▼
<b>3 · The audience it finds</b>
   More people who complete lead forms cheaply:
   the aspirational, the reflexive, the free-thing-collectors.
   <i>Cost per lead goes DOWN. This looks like progress.</i>
        │
        ▼
<b>4 · The outcome</b>
   Lead volume up · cost per lead down · revenue flat.
   <i>It was never told a purchase exists.</i>
        │
        ▼
        └──────▶ back to 2, with worse data, cheaper leads,
                and a dashboard that looks better every week.
</pre>

Step 2 is where the intuition usually breaks. People imagine the algorithm as a slightly dim assistant that failed to understand what they meant. It is the opposite. It understood perfectly and it is winning. You asked for cheap form completions and it is finding you cheaper and cheaper form completions. The metric you are steering by improves every month right up until someone asks what the revenue did.

<div class="callout">
  <p><b>The diagnostic tell.</b> If your cost per lead has been improving month over month and your revenue has not moved, you are not getting better at marketing. You are getting better at finding people who will say yes to nothing. Those are different skills and only one of them is worth money.</p>
</div>

### The fix, as five moves

1. **Instrument the purchase.** The purchase event must fire back to the ad platform with the identifiers that let it be matched to a click. If purchases live in a CRM or a payment processor and never travel back to the ad account, the algorithm cannot learn from them, and everything below is impossible.
2. **Change the objective.** Move the campaign objective from lead-form completions to the purchase event. Nothing else about the campaign needs to change on day one.
3. **Expect the front number to get worse.** Cost per lead will rise, often sharply. Lead volume will fall. This is the system correctly declining to buy you people who were never going to pay.
4. **Watch the back number instead.** Cost per customer is the metric under management now. If cost per lead doubles and cost per customer falls, you have won.
5. **Give it enough events to learn from.** Optimising toward an event the account almost never sees produces noise, not learning. High-ticket B2B, long sales cycles, anything with a human in the loop: the purchase event is too rare and too late. That is why the fallback table in step 4 exists: when purchases are too rare, optimise on the nearest event that costs the person effort. Effort is the proxy for intent, and you want the earliest event in your funnel that still requires some.

### Why cost per qualified conversation is worth reporting

Cost per lead answers "what did it cost to make someone appear in my CRM." Cost per qualified conversation cannot be gamed by removing friction: every trick that drives cost per lead down either leaves it unchanged or makes it worse, because it adds unqualified volume without adding conversations. Attribution will not be perfect, and it does not need to be. The number is for comparing your own months and your own channels against each other, not for winning an argument about attribution windows.

Two things happen when you start reporting it. Cheap channels stop looking cheap. And the argument in your team shifts from "how do we get more leads" to "which of these people is worth a conversation," which is a much better argument to be having.

### Small-market maths

A €1,000 test returned 36x ROAS (return on ad spend: 36 euros of revenue for every euro of ads). It is real, and it is the kind of number that gets you booked on podcasts. It was a small market, Latvia, under two million people. The product-market fit was unusually good, in a way I noticed rather than engineered. The timing was right. If I ran the same playbook with the same competence into a large, competitive, well-served market, I would not get anything close, because the mechanism that produced the result is mostly not repeatable by effort.

**Why small markets flatter you.** In a market of under two million people, a €1,000 budget buys meaningful reach: actual coverage of a real slice of the people you could sell to. In a large market the same €1,000 disappears into a rounding error and the algorithm never gets out of its learning phase. Same money, same ads, same operator: one produces a case study and one produces noise. Competition in the auction is thinner, so attention is cheaper. And when product-market fit is genuinely good, a small market lets you find the responsive part of it quickly enough that the whole thing looks like a repeatable system rather than a fortunate encounter.

**Why small markets punish you, for exactly the same reason.** There is a floor on how many people exist. Frequency spikes fast, because the pool of people to show ads to is finite and you reach the end of it. The same audience sees the same creative until it stops working, and no amount of budget produces new people. Scaling does not mean spending more. It means making new creative, finding a new segment, or leaving the country. The ceiling arrives early and it is hard.

<div class="callout">
  <p><b>The rule this gives you.</b> When a result is spectacular, ask what about the <i>conditions</i> produced it before you ask what about your <i>execution</i> produced it. Market size, competitive density, product-market fit and timing routinely swamp skill. Attributing a 36x to your own judgement is the same error as attributing €0.38 to your targeting. Both numbers are facts about a situation, not about a marketer.</p>
</div>

### The LIFT emails, with the weak number left in

A cold email programme for the LIFT digitalisation programme: 4,837 emails sent, roughly 50% open rate, a 5% reply rate against a B2B standard of 1 to 3%, 127 replies, roughly 70% positive. The open rate is the weakest number in that list and I would not defend it: question 6 applies to me as much as to anyone, and opens are increasingly measured by machines rather than people. The number that matters is 127 replies: a human choosing to spend a minute writing back, which is a real cost, freely paid. Seventy percent of them being positive means the list and the message were aimed at people who had the problem. Same operator, same year as the €0.38 campaign. The difference is not effort or cleverness. One counted an action that cost the other person something, and one counted an action that did not.
